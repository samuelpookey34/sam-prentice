The test — applied to all three

There are three levers, and every one of them gets run against the same three rules, in this order: don't lose money, make money, save on taxes. Taxes are third, not first. The fastest way to pay no tax is to lose the money, so a lever that fails rule one doesn't get used no matter how good the tax number looks. That's the whole filter, and it's why two of the three below survive and one doesn't.

Lever 1 — change the entity responsible

Income that lands on your personal return is taxed at your personal rate. Route it through a different entity — a trust, a C-corp, a charitable remainder trust — and it's taxed under a different set of rules before it ever reaches your 1040. Harder on pure W-2 than on business income, but not closed off.

Don't lose moneyNothing is spent. The capital stays yours, it just answers to different rules.
Make moneyThe money keeps working inside the structure while it's there.
Save on taxesTaxed under the entity's rules instead of your marginal rate.
Passes all three — worth modeling

Lever 2 — buy assets that create losses

Real estate, oil and gas, an operating business — anything you materially participate in. The goal is paper losses from depreciation, not real ones: you keep the asset and its cash flow while the deduction offsets income. Your wife's business losses already worked exactly this way against your W-2.

Don't lose moneyYou still own the asset. The loss is on paper, not in your pocket.
Make moneyOnly if it stands on its own as an investment first — that's the bar I hold it to.
Save on taxesDepreciation offsets income while the asset keeps producing.
Passes all three — most likely fit

Lever 3 — buy coupons

Leveraged charitable gifts, conservation easements, the things marketed hardest. Heavy marketing is exactly what draws the IRS in — they wait for volume, then work through the list. You called these too risky before I did. You were right.

Don't lose moneyFails here, and that ends it. If the coupon isn't honored in three years, you owe the tax and the money you paid is long gone.
Make moneyThere's no asset to own and nothing to earn. You bought a piece of paper.
Save on taxesOnly if it holds up — which is the one thing you can't know when you buy it.
Fails rule one — I don't do these

The short-term rental idea only solves half your problem

STR works off bonus depreciation, which the One Big Beautiful Bill brought back federally. California doesn't conform. So an STR can mitigate the federal side while your 13.3% keeps right on going — which is why it gets recommended to Californians who then wonder why their state bill didn't move. If we use it, we pair it with a separate California strategy.

The wind-down: one real deduction, one wishful one

The donated bags are a genuine charitable deduction and flow nicely against W-2 income — charitable intent makes the tax game easier, not harder. The $50–60K of equity is the wishful one: in a pass-through, that money most likely already reduced your income on the way in, and the same dollar can't be deducted twice. Worth confirming against the returns, but plan on zeroing out rather than a second loss.
